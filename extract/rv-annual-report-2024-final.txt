Chairman’s Report
I am pleased to present to you Racing Victoria’s (RV)
2024 Annual Report, which gives a detailed oversight
of the company’s key metrics during the 2023-24
financial year (FY24) and also provides a broader
view of the performance of the Victorian racing
industry across the 12-month period.
Whilst the year was not without its challenges there
is no doubt that, thanks to the support of our clubs
and participants and the commitment of our staff
members, now capably led by Chief Executive Aaron
Morrison and his Executive team, the industry is on
a very firm footing, and we have every reason to be
confident of its stability and prosperity as we look to
the future.
That confidence is built partly upon the $2 billion
industry funding deal which marked the start of
a new era. The favourable terms of the industry’s
landmark 10-year agreement with the State
Government, which was executed upon conclusion of
the Tabcorp Joint Venture (VicTAB JV), provided all
three codes of racing with much greater certainty as
we plan for the next decade and beyond.
Wagering is the financial lifeblood of the industry
and although total turnover was down from the $9.16
billon peak experienced in FY22, significantly the
FY24 figure of $7.9 billion remained approximately
12% above pre-COVID levels.
Allied to wagering, racing’s success is also dependent
on our ability to grow our audience share in an
increasingly competitive sporting landscape, and so it
was heartening to see attendance figures climb year-
on-year during the 2023 Spring Racing Carnival.
The introduction of the Victorian Country Cups
Series, which culminated in a fitting finale on the new
Caulfield Thousand Day, was a significant factor in
this growth as regional racegoers flocked to country
tracks right across the state to enjoy a relaxed and
informal day at the races.
One of the most significant changes from a club
perspective occurred when, following a members’
vote in December 2023, Pakenham and Cranbourne
Racing Clubs merged to become Southside Racing.
The creation of this ‘super club’ delivers increased
benefits to members and participants, efficiencies
and improved commercial outcomes for the club,
and an opportunity to further build and promote the
sport in Melbourne’s south east.
During FY24 we increased our investment in
participant wellbeing and the management of
WorkCover costs, which were having a significant
impact on the ongoing viability of many training
stables. The initiatives, which were introduced
following extensive consultation with the Australian
Trainers’ Association and a range of stakeholder
groups, brought the industry’s support for trainer and
stable staff wellbeing into line with our commitment
to jockey and equine welfare.
There is still more work to be done in the wellbeing
space, and the findings of the independent review
undertaken by the Office of the Racing Integrity
Commissioner led to an increased commitment by
RV to deliver safe, respectful and inclusive work
environments for all.
Our care also extends to all racehorses in Victoria,
both during and after their careers, which is why
we introduced our new Thoroughbred Racehorse
Welfare Policy from May 2024. The code of practice,
which is enforceable under the Australian Rules
of Racing, defines the minimum standards and
guidelines for caring for horses.
In addition, RV introduced a Local Rule of Racing
which prohibits industry participants from sending
live thoroughbreds to knackeries or abattoirs, and
we also increased our visibility of retired racehorses
via our Off The Track Community, with more than
7,000 members claiming over 5,000 retired Victorian
thoroughbred racehorses to better understand
their movements.
FY24 was notable for some high-profile retirements
in the Victorian jockey ranks, with Melbourne Cup
winners Damien Oliver, Brett Prebble and Michelle
Payne – whose name still appears in the racebook
alongside her brother and training partner Patrick –
all calling time on their decorated riding careers.
‘Ollie’ was in the audience at the Victorian Racing
Awards to celebrate the state’s leading rider
for 2023-24, as Blake Shinn collected a second
successive Scobie Breasley Medal to cap another
stellar season in the saddle. The other main winner on
the night was Without A Fight, whose completion of
the coveted Cups double enabled Team Freedman to
collect three awards.
It would be remiss of me not to mention the sterling
contributions to Victorian racing over an extended
period made by Mike Hirst and Greg Nichols, who
both retired from the RV Board during FY24. I would
also like to thank former CEO Andrew Jones for his
contribution to RV.
We welcomed Tim Rourke and Mark Player to the RV
Board – both bringing extensive racing and breeding
knowledge, along with business acumen across
various industries. Their fresh sets of eyes have
already been beneficial.
I also want to acknowledge and thank the Minister
for Racing, The Hon. Anthony Carbines and Shadow
Minister for Racing, The Hon. Tim Bull, who are both
passionate about seeing racing prosper, and have
engaged in constructive relationships with RV and
the broader racing industry.
They both recognise the racing industry is a key
economic contributor, particularly in regional areas,
to the state and sustains the employment of more
than 25,000 full-time equivalent jobs for Victorians.
Finally, I’d like to take this opportunity to thank my
fellow Board members and RV’s Executive team
and broader staff for their outstanding efforts
and contributions over the past 12 months, and
to applaud all those that have participated in or
engaged with racing, allowing us to deliver the
best racing in Australia for punters and fans to enjoy.
Yours sincerely,
Tim Eddy

CEO’s Report
As always, wagering and engagement was at its
strongest during the peak period of the Spring
Racing Carnival, with all 15 of the highest turnover
races in Victoria during FY24 run at metropolitan
meetings during October and November 2023.
For the first time the carnival was extended deeper
into November with the introduction of Caulfield
Thousand Day, headlined by two Group 1 contests
in the Thousand Guineas and the Sir Rupert Clarke
Stakes. This new fixture produced $73 million in
turnover (around 20% more than the comparable
meeting held on the same date in the prior year) and
attracted strong starter numbers.
Total expenditure for the financial year fell to $548.0
million from $554.0 million in FY23. A key focus on
reviewing and reducing operational costs across the
RV Group, including our media businesses, led to a
$7 million net year-on-year reduction ($12 million
gross reduction before wage inflation and contract
indexation). This will continue to be a focus for the
business into this next year.
Significantly, despite declining wagering revenue,
the reduction in RV’s operational costs helped the
industry to maintain prizemoney and bonuses on
offer at similar levels, that being $316.3 million in
FY24, compared to $318.0 million in FY23.
Assisted by the expansion of the VOBIS program,
which now offers in excess of $30 million in
prizemoney, bonuses and vouchers each season,
the total amount available to racing participants
in Victoria has now grown by $144.3 million – or
83.9% – over the past decade, with RV committing
to maintain the current levels of prizemoney and
bonuses on offer into FY25.
FY24 saw the successful trial of 10-race Saturday
metropolitan meetings, which generated gross
wagering revenue of $5 million and supported the
payment of an incremental $2.8 million in prizemoney
to a range of participants and owners. With the
support of participants, a 10th race has been
scheduled again in the 2024-25 season for a reduced
period of eight months.
This, and other initiatives, helped ensure that Victoria
maintained its position as a national leader for the
average prizemoney and bonuses on offer per TAB
race in FY24.
The areas of welfare, integrity, media and
infrastructure remain integral pillars of RV’s business
model, and you can find detailed reports on each
within the Annual Report.
Another priority is to maintain racing’s social licence
to operate, underpinned by a communal commitment
to each and every horse’s welfare before, during and
after its racing career.
Our range of Off The Track programs continue to
promote the virtues of the thoroughbred and support
vulnerable horses, whilst our pioneering veterinary
protocols – which helped to ensure a fatality-free
running of the Melbourne Cup for the third successive
year – play a key role in our efforts around injury
reduction and safety, and help ensure we continue
to meet the minimum standards expected by the
industry and broader community.
Our industry-owned media business, Racing.com
Media, has played a key role in showcasing racing
and has been a key driver of engagement with
Victorian racing nationally, and a strong contributor
to wagering turnover. The business continues to drive
growth in profitability, as well as media rights income,
which directly supports our clubs. This business is an
important asset for the industry which will continue
to evolve as we seek to further elevate the promotion
of racing, including celebrating our stars – both
human and equine – the rich heritage of our clubs,
events and racing carnivals and the significant social
and economic contribution made by Victorian racing.
Ensuring we maximise use of industry resources to
broaden the coverage and promotion of Victorian
racing to a wider audience is a key priority going
forward, as is leveraging our collective industry
experience and growing a culture of collaboration,
both internally and with our external stakeholders.
Our future success and ability to continue to grow
and ensure a sustainable industry for our participants
and those who invest in our industry, is dependent on
the willingness of all participants and clubs to unite
and support the industry to keep moving forward.
I wish to recognise and thank each and every
industry participant, along with our metropolitan and
country clubs for their important contributions and
continued efforts to ensure that Victoria maintains its
position as the country’s premium racing product.
Finally, my thanks also go to the RV Board, now led
by Chairman Tim Eddy, for their vision and support;
and to my Executive team and all RV staff members
for their dedication and commitment in the cause of
ensuring the pre-eminence of Victorian racing for the
benefit of all of its stakeholders.
Yours sincerely,
Aaron Morrison
Chief Executive Officer
It gives me great pleasure to share for the first time
in my role as Chief Executive, the performance of
RV and the key activities undertaken to support
the continued pre-eminence of the Victorian
thoroughbred racing industry during FY24.
Our statutory reported net deficit of $11.8 million,
compared to a net surplus of $0.2 million in the
previous financial year. The reported result includes
one-off industry support costs relating to the
Southside Racing merger, and other non-recurring
costs. Excluding these costs, the underlying net
deficit was only $3.0 million, which is $9.1 million
better than our initial budgeted deficit of $12.1
million that had pre-empted significant changes
in the wagering market and to industry funding
arrangements, as we transitioned from the expiring
wagering licence and VicTAB Joint Venture
(VicTAB JV) to new arrangements from FY25.
Whilst broader macro-economic challenges
led to an ongoing decline in wagering, thereby
negatively impacting RV’s revenue streams, priority
was given to maintaining returns to the industry
through prizemoney and bonuses, and ongoing
investments in important areas including welfare and
infrastructure.
Whilst we saw a 10.2% decline in wagering turnover
to $7.9 billion, in a market which is declining overall,
turnover remains above pre-COVID levels. A number
of factors contributed to the decline in wagering
turnover, including cost-of-living pressures, high
interest rates, increased competition from sports, a
reduction in advertising and customer promotions
from Wagering Service Providers (WSPs), and
additional wagering regulatory measures introduced
by Government. However, we were fortunate to have
otherwise benefitted from stronger than average
wagering operator gross margins, which drove
strong race fields product fee income and offset
the turnover decline. Consequently, total wagering
revenue in FY24 increased by 1.0% to $364.4 million.
The total revenue from operations decreased by
3.1% to $536.8 million, due in large part to the end of
some State Government funding programs ahead of
the transition to a new industry funding framework,
and the loss of associated income from those
Government sources.
Whilst distributions from the VicTAB JV continued
to decline in their final year (down a further 6.9%
year-on-year), we were able to proactively mitigate
some of this impact through a negotiated exit from
the historical joint venture with Tabcorp ahead of
the transition to a new Victorian wagering licence.
To ensure a more favourable outcome and provide
the industry with greater certainty of its financial
position, the two parties agreed to fixed payments
for the final months of the expiring VicTAB JV. The
historic joint venture between the Victorian Racing
Industry and Tabcorp has now since ended in August
2024 after 30 years, and the industry funding model
has transitioned to new arrangements that provide
the industry with stability and certainty going
forwards, whilst creating a ‘level playing field’ for all
wagering operators, including Tabcorp.
This is a truly significant change and the ‘end of an
era’ in industry funding partnership arrangements
dating back to the original privatisation of the Tote
by the Victorian Government in 1994. The VicTAB JV
has been an incredible part of our history, strength
and success and I wish to acknowledge and thank
Tabcorp for their support, whilst we look forward to
developing a strong new partnership, more reflective
of the contemporary wagering industry. We also
thank and acknowledge all of our wagering partners
for their ongoing support of Victorian thoroughbred
racing, making it the number one product of choice
for punters nationwide.

Racing & Programming 	• 	Good Friday racing moved to the Yarra Valley
as the Country Discovery Raceday continued
its journey around Victoria, following
previous visits to Sale (2022) and Geelong
(2023). Headlined by the Country Discovery
(1100m), which at $500,000 is regional
Victoria’s richest sprint race, the meeting will
head to Echuca in 2025. For the first time
Cranbourne also hosted a dual code fixture
on Good Friday, with the night meeting
incorporating a host of fundraising initiatives
for the Good Friday Appeal, including the
inaugural Southside Challenge.
• 	The Listed Sportsbet Pakenham Cup
(2500m) was switched from its traditional
December date and run under the lights on
Friday, 15 March 2024. The $300,000 contest
was won by Ashrun, who was Group 1
placed at his next start in the Tancred Stakes
(2400m).
• 	Between August 2023 and May 2024, Racing
Victoria (RV) conducted a 10-month trial
of 10-race Saturday metropolitan meetings,
which generated gross wagering revenue of
$5 million. This supported the payment of
an additional $2.8 million in prizemoney to
a range of participants and owners, with the
balance invested back into the industry.
An additional 34 Saturday metropolitan
races were run as part of the trial, with
average field sizes noticeably higher than
meeting averages. Those races were won by
27 different trainers and 28 jockeys, ensuring
the opportunities and prizemoney were
widely distributed. Following a review of the
trial, RV announced that 10-race Saturday
metropolitan meetings would return in the
2024-25 season for a reduced period of
eight months.
Spring Racing Carnival
History was made during the 2023 Spring Racing
Carnival, as Romantic Warrior became the first horse
trained in Hong Kong to win the Ladbrokes Cox Plate,
whilst Without A Fight became the 12th stayer to
complete the coveted Cups double as the Freedman
name was once again etched onto Australian racing’s
most recognisable trophy.
After arriving with a big reputation, Romantic Warrior
perhaps fell short of those lofty expectations when
he could only manage fourth on his Australian
debut in the Group 1 TAB Turnbull Stakes (2000m)
at Flemington. But trainer Danny Shum and regular
rider James McDonald never lost faith in his abilities,
and their belief was vindicated when he produced
a heart-stopping victory in Australasia’s weight-for-
age championship, defeating Mr Brightside by the
narrowest of margins with Alligator Blood a further
neck away in third.
Either side of that stirring duel, Without A Fight
produced a pair of performances which elevated
him to rare company as he galloped into the history
books for Anthony and Sam Freedman. Having
claimed the Carlton Draught Caulfield Cup (2400m)
at the expense of his former stablemate West Wind
Blows, the import became the first horse for more
than two decades to add the Lexus Melbourne Cup
(3200m) in the same season thanks to another
masterful ride by Mark Zahra.
If Without A Fight was the undisputed king of the
staying ranks, the sprinting division was dominated
by a queen of the turf as Imperatriz compiled a
flawless spring campaign which culminated in her
third successive Group 1 win in the Darley Champions
Sprint (1200m) on the final day of the famous
Melbourne Cup Carnival.
Festival of Racing
Three months later Imperatriz
promptly picked up where she
left off, marking a successful
return to Flemington with
victory in the Group 1 Black
Caviar Lightning (1000m)
before signing off her
career in her adopted home
state with a 10th – and final
– success at racing’s highest
level in the 3 Point Motors
William Reid Stakes (1200m).
The autumn months also saw
another mare in Pride Of Jenni
enhance her reputation with a
stunning win in the Sharp EIT All-Star Mile.
The daring ‘catch me if you can’ tactics employed by
Declan Bates had reaped spectacular reward during
the spring, when Pride Of Jenni captured back-to-
back Group 1 wins over the Flemington mile, and the
pair combined again to take out one of the Festival
of Racing’s biggest prizes with another display of
sustained speed and stamina.
During FY24, 4,324 races were held in Victoria across
538 meetings. A total of 41,821 race starts were
made by 9,081 horses, with races at TAB meetings
attracting an average field size of 9.7 starters. That
figure rose to 11.0 starters for the 191 races conducted
at premier meetings, and to 9.9 across the 40 races
at the four super premier meetings.
New Initiatives
For the first time the Schweppes Thousand Guineas
(1600m) and the Neds Sir Rupert Clarke Stakes
(1400m) were moved to the back end of the Spring
Racing Carnival, as Caulfield successfully staged
a new Group 1 raceday on Saturday, 18 November
2023. The two feature races were won respectively
by Joliestar (Chris Waller) and Magic Time (Grahame
Begg), both of whom subsequently franked the form
by adding further Stakes success to their CVs.
The undercard included the inaugural $500,000
Country Cups Final (2000m), which brought
together the best-performed horses from Victoria’s
55 Country Cups. Gemma Rielly’s Claidheamh Mor
delivered the biggest win of his emerging trainer’s
career when he powered home under Carleen Hefel
to collect a cheque for $275,000.
Other racing and programming initiatives rolled out
in FY24 include:
• 	Super Saturday on the Coast made its debut
at Geelong in January, with a healthy crowd
of almost 5,000 turning out to see Holymanz
win the inaugural running of the $300,000
bet365 Coastal Classic (1700m) for Ciaron
Maher. Stay Focused ran away with the Rex
Gorell Geelong Diamond (1100m) to secure
qualification for the Group 1 Sportsbet Blue
Diamond Stakes (1200m) at Caulfield, where
he finished fifth – beaten just over a length –
behind Hayasugi.
• 	For the first time the TAB Australian Cup
(2000m) was held two weeks after the
Sharp EIT All-Star Mile (1600m), giving the
country’s premier middle-distance horses
the opportunity to compete for a combined
prize pool of $7 million. Cascadian took full
advantage of the revised program, using his
minor placing behind Pride Of Jenni in the
All-Star Mile as the springboard to launch a
successful defence of his Australian Cup title
at Flemington a fortnight later.
• 	In a revamp to the All-Star Mile qualification
process, eight ‘win and you’re in’ races were
held in the lead-up to the sixth edition of
the $4 million contest, which returned to
Caulfield under its annual rotation policy.
The automatic qualifiers included the Magic
Millions 3YO Guineas (1400m) on the Gold
Coast, as well as the two feature mile races
held on New Zealand’s richest raceday, the
TAB Karaka Millions 3YO Classic (1600m) and
the Elsdon Park Aotearoa Classic (1600m).
The latter was won by Desert Lightning,
who ran ninth behind Pride Of Jenni in the
All-Star Mile.

Integrity Services
RV’s Integrity Services Department is central to a
healthy and prosperous Victorian racing industry
and sits at the heart of racing’s ‘right to exist’.
Equine Sample Collection
A total of 13,029 equine samples were collected
during the 2023-24 season, with prohibited
substances detected in 13 samples (0.1%) – down
from 18 (0.14%) in the 2022-23 season.
A total of 1,220 equine samples were collected as
part of RV’s out of competition testing program.
Safety Standards
In 2023, RV’s core safety standards for horses
competing in the Spring Racing Carnival remained
for the Spring Racing Carnival with a focus on
further enhancing administrative processes for their
connections.
The safety standards have made a notable difference
across the past three Spring Racing Carnivals which
were conducted without serious injuries.
Equine Limb Injury Prevention
Program
RV, in partnership with the Victorian Government
and the University of Melbourne, continued its
investment in FY24 into its Equine Limb Injury
Prevention Program, which has direct benefits for
Victorian racehorses.
A key result of the research program was the
construction and use of the PET scanner at the
University of Melbourne Equine Centre – helping
improve the early detection and prevention of limb
injuries in racehorses.
Safety Record
The core objective of RV’s research investment is to
reduce the rate of raceday fatalities in racing towards
0.00%. Victoria’s raceday fatality rate for flat racing is
among the lowest in world racing.
In the 2023-24 season, there were 23 fatalities in flat
racing in Victoria from 41,821 starts. This is equivalent
to a fatality rate of 0.05%, which is the same as the
2022-23 season.
Equally, efforts to reduce the rate of incidents in
jumps racing continue through education, equipment
upgrades and policy enforcement.
In the 2024 season, which ran from March to August,
there were seven fatalities in jumps races in Victoria
from 573 starters. That is equivalent to a fatality rate
of 1.2%.
Jumps Racing Review
RV announced on 26 September 2024, that its annual
review of the Victorian jumps racing season would
go beyond that of the standard process given the
increase in fatal incidents across the 2024 jumps
racing season.
The review is being conducted by a newly constituted
Jumps Review Group (JRG) chaired by RV’s
Executive General Manager – Integrity, Jamie Stier,
and supported by former Victorian Racing Integrity
Board Chair and former Supreme Court Judge,
Justice Jack Forrest, as an independent member.
The report and recommendations will consider the
future viability of jumps racing in Victoria, and/or
what further changes can be made to improve the
safety record of the sport.
The JRG will provide its report and recommendations
to the RV Executive by the end of November
2024 who will then provide the report and
recommendations to the RV Board for decision by
the end of December 2024.
The Racing Integrity Commissioner, Sean
Carroll, will also receive a copy of the report and
recommendations to review the approach undertaken
by the JRG and RV Executive.
RV will publish a summary of the recommendations
as soon as practicable following the Board’s decision.
Thoroughbred Racehorse Welfare
Policy
The Thoroughbred Racehorse Welfare Policy
(TRWP) defines the minimum standards and best
practice guidelines for caring for and interacting with
thoroughbred racehorses in Victoria.
The TRWP came into effect from 1 May 2024 and is
enforceable under Australian Rules of Racing 233
(a) – a person must not breach a policy, regulation or
code of practice published by Racing Australia or a
Principal Racing Authority.
The TRWP promotes good welfare and considers
each horse’s mental and physical health; supervision;
housing; exercise and training; transport; and
retirement and rehoming.
While most stables already meet and exceed the
TRWP, it ensures participants (especially trainers)
address any required improvements.
Local Rule of Racing 94C
The RV Board endorsed the introduction of Local
Rule of Racing 94C to prohibit industry participants
sending live thoroughbreds to knackeries or abattoirs
from 1 February 2024.
The decision to implement the rule was made by the
RV Board only after it was satisfied that the range
of world class post-racing transition programs put
in place over the past five years provided genuine
options to support all healthy thoroughbreds to
transition to successful second careers.

Wagering turnover on Victorian Thoroughbred
Racing (VTR) fell by 10% year-on-year in FY24,
with the decline driven by challenging economic
circumstances, which is intensifying competition
for wagering share as well as discretionary spend
generally. Turnover was also negatively impacted by
a sustained period of high margins for the Wagering
Service Providers (WSPs) on VTR, which was largely
driven by unfavourable results for customers.
Free bet turnover was significantly down year-on-
year by 20%, which was somewhat expected after
the significant generosity spend of FY23 due in part
to the entry of Betr into the wagering market. In a
declining turnover environment, WSPs sought to
protect revenue which in some cases necessitated
a decrease in generosity spend. This was observed
through much of the second half of the year, with
meetings that formerly attracted money back
specials on all races having promotions decreased
to eight races, six races and, in the case of some
midweek meetings, no races at all.
Despite challenging wagering conditions, VTR
retained much of the growth in customers it enjoyed
during the COVID-19 period. Data gathered from the
major WSPs indicates that active accounts remained
stable in FY24, but bet count and average bet size
had both declined. In comparison to FY20, turnover
remains 12% higher, whilst free bet turnover remains
60% higher.

REVIEW OF OPERATIONS
The net deficit of the consolidated entity for the financial year ended 30 June 2024 was $11.834 million
compared to a surplus of $0.163 million in the prior year.
The result was materially in line with expectations, with the consolidated entity budgeting for a net deficit
of over $12.000 million. Whilst macroeconomic challenges (impacting wagering) and the cessation of
Government funding arrangements (towards prizemoney) were forecast to impact key revenue sources,
priority was still given to maintaining returns to the industry through owners, participants, and clubs. Industry
sustainability funds accumulated over recent years were utilised to support the operating financial position
and maintenance of important industry support through an expected period of operational uncertainty.
Revenue
The total revenue from operations decreased by -3.1% to $536.755 million versus the prior year (2023:
$553.771 million).
Total wagering turnover, the key indicator of wagering engagement and activity levels, was $7.927 billion
(2023: $8.820 billion) and was down 10.2% on the prior year. This was reflective of ongoing macroeconomic
challenges including rising cost of living expenses and interest rate rises, as well as the impacts of regulatory
reforms such as the introduction of BetStop (national self-exclusion register) and the prohibition of deposits
via credit cards. Total wagering revenue for the full year increased by $3.493 million (+1.0%) versus the prior
year. Whilst a net uplift was predominantly due to strong wagering operating margins compared to both the
prior year and short-term historic averages which drove an uplift in race fields income, the overall wagering
market remained challenged.
Included within the wagering revenue result are the VicTAB Joint Venture (JV) distributions which declined by
-6.9%. This downturn in VicTAB profitability was a more favourable outcome than would have otherwise been
produced in the normal course, due to the JV exit arrangements agreed to between the Victorian Racing
Industry (VRI) and Tabcorp during the period. Under the terms of an agreement executed by the parties
on 1 March 2024, the parties agreed to fixed payments for the final months of the expiring joint venture,
which provided the VRI with greater certainty of its financial outcomes. New industry funding arrangements
associated with the end of the historic JV, commencing from 1 July 2024 are covered below under subsequent
events.
Funding received through various Victorian Government arrangements included a share of payments made to
the VRI from the Victorian Government’s collection of Point of Consumption Tax (PoCT) levied on wagering
by Victorian residents. Victorian Racing Industry Funding (VRIF) grants were also received to partially
fund infrastructure projects during the period and to support initiatives including the equine welfare limb
injury prevention program and the VOBIS platinum voucher program. Revenue from Government funding
decreased by $21.145 million (-25.4%) on prior year, largely due to the 31 December 2022 cessation of a
multi-year commitment to support increases in thoroughbred prizemoney, as well as a reduction in scheduled
racecourse infrastructure project work and a decline in PoCT receipts linked to wagering.
Media revenue increased by $1.879 million (+2.2%) to $86.696 million. The uplift primarily related to increased
advertising revenue and channel sponsorship generated by Racing.com and media rights income generated
via international markets.
Funds under investment (excluding withdrawals) increased in value over the period due to continued strong
interest income and growth in major financial markets. Total investment revenue, including realised gains/
losses, unrealised gains/losses and investment income in the form of dividends and interest was positive for
the year at +$9.091 million, which was similar to the performance in the prior year (2023: +$9.014 million).

operations. Management continues to pursue cost control and process efficiency improvements across all key
activities.

across the Spring Racing Carnival & Festival of Racing, hosting the Asian Racing Conference in March 2023
(noting associated revenue offsets), and decreased administration and technology expenditure.
These were partially offset by non-recurring or non-Business as Usual legal and regulatory costs associated
with the NSW Supreme Court matter and recommendations stemming from the ORIC review into the VRI
victim support and complaint processes.
Total operational expenditure has declined significantly on prior year despite wage increases, contracted cost
indexation and significant inflationary pressures. The consolidated entity has maintained solid control over
operational expenditure in recent years, keeping average annual increases below high inflation levels. This is
reflected by a five-year compound average growth rate of 3%.
Depreciation, Amortisation and Other Asset Expenditure
Depreciation, amortisation and other asset expenditure increased by $7.194 million (+89.1%) to $15.266
million. This expenditure increase relates to the loss on disposal associated with the agreed value transfer of
Pakenham stabling allotments and Cranbourne land to the Cranbourne Pakenham Racing Club as part of the
combination arrangements agreed between Racing Victoria and the merged clubs.
Investment Activities
Racing Victoria has agreed to hold and invest financial assets on behalf of the VTRI as part of an Industry
Sustainability Fund (ISF). The purpose of the ISF is to ensure that the industry retains sufficient funds to
protect the long-term sustainability of the industry, support the operating financial position and maintenance
of important industry support during times of operational uncertainty, provide funds to support important
industry-wide capital investment and to assist with finance and investment that may not be otherwise
available.
The ISF framework defines its assets as those investments which have a broader industry benefit with an
investment or commercial return objective, interest bearing loans with related parties (at discount to market
rates), and land earmarked for development with a commercial return.
The consolidated entity engages Evans and Partners to manage and invest accumulated surplus cash that
sits outside operating cash requirements. A balanced investment mandate is maintained, with investments
made across various asset classes including cash, interest rate securities, Australian and international equities,
property, infrastructure and alternative investments. The investment mandate and asset allocations are
reviewed on an annual basis together with the governing investment policy.

24. Material Accounting Policies
The following significant accounting policies have been adopted in the preparation and presentation of the
financial report:
(a) 	Borrowings and Borrowing Costs
Borrowings are recorded initially at fair value, net of transaction costs. Subsequent to initial
recognition, borrowings are measured at amortised cost with any difference between the initial
recognised amount and the redemption value being recognised in profit and loss over the period of
the borrowing using the effective interest rate method.
Other borrowing costs are recognised as an expense in the period in which they are incurred.
(b) 	Cash and Cash Equivalents
Cash comprises cash on hand and demand deposits. Cash equivalents are short term, highly liquid
investments that are readily convertible to known amounts of cash and are subject to an insignificant
risk of changes in value.
(c) 	Employee Benefits
A liability is recognised for benefits accruing to employees in respect of wages and salaries, annual
leave, long service leave, and sick leave when it is probable that settlement will be required, and they
are capable of being measured reliably.
Liabilities recognised in respect of employee benefits expected to be settled within 12 months are
measured at their nominal values, using the remuneration rate expected to apply at the time of
settlement.
Liabilities recognised in respect of employee benefits which are not expected to be settled within
12 months are measured as the present value of the estimated future cash outflows to be made by the
consolidated entity in respect of services provided by employees up to the reporting date.
(d) 	Financial assets
Classification and measurement of financial assets
All recognised financial assets that are within the scope of AASB 9 are required to be subsequently
measured at amortised cost or fair value on the basis of the entity’s business model for managing the
financial assets and the contractual cash flow characteristics of the financial assets. Specifically:
• 	debt investments that are held within a business model whose objective is to collect the contractual
cash flows, and that have contractual cash flows that are solely payments of principal and interest on
the principal amount outstanding, are subsequently measured at amortised cost;
• 	all other debt investments and equity investments are subsequently measured at fair value through
profit and loss (FVTPL).
Despite the foregoing, the consolidated entity may make the following irrevocable election/
designation at initial recognition of a financial asset:
• 	the consolidated entity may irrevocably elect to present subsequent changes in fair value of an equity
investment that is neither held for trading nor contingent consideration recognised by an acquirer in
a business combination to which IFRS 3 applies in other comprehensive income; and
• 	the consolidated entity may irrevocably designate a debt investment that meets the amortised cost
or fair value through other comprehensive income (FVTOCI) criteria as measured at FVTPL if doing
so eliminates or significantly reduces an accounting mismatch.
Debt instruments that are subsequently measured at amortised cost or at FVTOCI are subject to
impairment testing.
Impairment of financial assets
In relation to the impairment of financial assets, AASB 9 requires an expected credit loss (ECL) model
as opposed to an incurred credit loss model under IAS 39. The ECL model requires the consolidated
entity to account for expected credit losses and changes in those expected credit losses at each
reporting date to reflect changes in credit risk since initial recognition of the financial assets. In other
words, it is no longer necessary for a credit event to have occurred before credit losses are recognised.
The consolidated entity reviewed its related party loan receivables for impairment under the General
Approach to determine whether there has been a Significant Increase in Credit Risk (SICR) since initial
recognition and whether loans are credit impaired. This determines whether the loans are in Stage 1,
Stage 2, or Stage 3, which in turn determines both;
• 	the amount of ECL to be recognised: 12-month ECL or Lifetime ECL; and
• 	the amount of interest income to be recognised in future reporting periods: Effective Interest Rate
(EIR) based on gross carrying amount of the loan which excludes ECL or the net carrying amount (i.e.
the amortised cost) which includes ECL.
Lifetime ECL are the ECL that result from all possible default events over the expected life of loans
whereas 12-month ECL are a portion of Lifetime ECL that represent the ECL that result from default
events that are possible within 12 months of the reporting date.
The consolidated entity reviewed its related party loans and assessed that none of the loans have
suffered SICR with no further credit losses recognised within 12 months of the reporting date.
Loss allowances for trade receivables are measured at an amount equal to lifetime ECLs. When
determining whether the credit risk of a financial asset has increased significantly since initial
recognition and when estimating ECLs, the consolidated entity considers reasonable and supportable
information that is relevant and available without undue cost or effort. This is determined by reference
to past default experience.

At each reporting date, the consolidated entity reviews the carrying amounts of its tangible and
intangible assets to determine whether there is any indication that those assets have suffered an
impairment loss. If any such indication exists, the recoverable amount of the asset is estimated to
determine the extent of the impairment loss (if any). Where the asset does not generate cash flows
that are independent from other assets, the consolidated entity estimates the recoverable amount of
the cash generating unit to which the asset belongs.
Where a reasonable and consistent basis can be identified, corporate assets are also allocated to
individual cash generating units, otherwise they are allocated the smallest group of cash generating
units for which a reasonable and consistent allocation basis can be identified.
Intangible assets with indefinite useful lives and intangible assets not yet available for use are tested

Recoverable amount is the higher of fair value less costs to sell and value in use. In assessing value in
use, the estimated future cash flows are discounted to their present value using a post-tax discount
rate that reflects current market assessments of the time value of money and the risks specific to
the asset for which the estimates of future cash flows have not been adjusted. Notwithstanding this,
value in use is depreciated replacement cost of an asset when the future economic benefits of the
asset are not primarily dependent on the asset’s ability to generate net cash inflows and where the
consolidated entity would, if deprived of the asset, replace its remaining future economic benefits. If
the recoverable amount of an asset (or cash-generating unit) is estimated to be less than it’s carrying
amount, the carrying amount of the asset (cash-generating unit) is reduced to its recoverable amount.
An impairment loss is recognised in profit or loss immediately, unless the relevant asset is carried at
fair value, in which case the impairment loss is treated as a revaluation decrease.
Where an impairment loss subsequently reverses, the carrying amount of the asset (cash-generating
unit) is increased to the revised estimate of its recoverable amount, but only to the extent that the
increased carrying amount does not exceed the carrying amount that would have been determined
had no impairment loss been recognised for the asset (cash-generating unit) in prior periods. A
reversal of an impairment loss is recognised in profit or loss immediately, unless the relevant asset
is carried at fair value, in which case the reversal of the impairment loss is treated as a revaluation
increase.

(o) 	Investments in Associates
