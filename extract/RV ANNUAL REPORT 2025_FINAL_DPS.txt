Chair’s Report
I am pleased to present to you Racing Victoria’s
(RV) 2025 Annual Report, which gives a detailed
oversight of the company’s key metrics during the
2024-25 Financial Year (FY25) and also provides a
broader view of the performance of the Victorian
Thoroughbred Racing Industry (VTRI) across the
12-month period.
The period was a substantive one as we transitioned
away from our historic Joint Venture agreement with
Tabcorp, which had underpinned our funding for
decades, to a new model which continues to recognise
our industry’s significant contribution to the state.
Our new funding arrangements include an increased
allocation of Point of Consumption Taxes (PoCT)
and a fixed annual contribution from the State
Government in recognition of the changes initiated
with the new Victorian Wagering Licence.
This investment in thoroughbred racing helps our
industry support 25,700 full-time equivalent jobs and
delivers $3.4 billion in value to the Victorian economy
each year.
Whilst these new arrangements provide future
certainty, the transition in funding models and the
impact of the broader economy on all forms of
wagering meant that FY25 ultimately resulted in
a small budgeted deficit for RV.
It was a period in which we prioritised the
maintenance of returns to industry, whilst working
to implement a business-wide efficiency program
to sustain that.
That strategy continues into FY26 where our efforts
to further reduce costs and create efficiencies, such
as bringing our media businesses, headlined by
Racing.com, back into the RV management structure
will continue to materialise.
In August 2024, we appointed Aaron Morrison as our
Chief Executive Officer after a four-month interim
period and an extended period prior as our Chief
Financial and Chief Operating Officer.
This move was complemented by an Executive Team
restructure which saw Vaughn Lynch, Peter Betson
and Andy Hoad join the business thereafter, whilst
Janelle Helleur was promoted from within.
Aaron, with the support of the Board and his
Executive Team, has made it a clear priority for
the business to strengthen our relationships with
stakeholders and participants, and this renewed focus
on those within our industry has been well received.
This has led to better collaboration and
communication across the industry which will
benefit Victorian racing as we strive to maximise
engagement in the highly competitive sports and
entertainment market.
As part of this, the Board and Aaron established a
Racing Sub-Committee with accompanying Racing
Forums, which have provided the platform for
industry leaders to share ideas and work together
collaboratively on key decisions and initiatives
around our racing program.
In his CEO’s Report, Aaron provides a detailed
overview of our financial results and outlines the
areas which will be a focus for the business into FY26
as we work towards returning to break-even, whilst
maintaining important industry priority investment.
Reflecting on 2024-25, I would like to acknowledge
the standout performers on our racetracks headlined
by Blake Shinn, who claimed a third successive
Scobie Breasley Medal, and Via Sistina, who was
crowned the 2025 Victorian Horse of the Year. Her
track record win in the 2024 Cox Plate is a memory
that will live long for many.
The Caulfield and Melbourne Cups in the spring of
2024 both delivered fairytale results for their jockeys
Harry Coffey (Duke De Sessa) and Robbie Dolan
(Knight’s Choice). Both riders have unique stories that
captured the hearts of the community and showed
that hard work and perseverance do reap rewards.
I want to pay tribute to my Deputy Chair Sharon
McCrohan who has been an invaluable member of
the RV Board over an eight-year term concluding in
September 2025. Sharon played an instrumental role
in helping ensure the continuation of Victorian racing
throughout the COVID pandemic, for which the
industry is forever grateful.
Whilst on the theme of retirements, I want to
acknowledge the significant contribution that Robbie
Griffiths has made as President of the Australian
Trainers Association over the past decade. We look
forward to working closely with his successor
Troy Corstens.
I also want to acknowledge and thank the Minister
for Racing, The Hon. Anthony Carbines, and Shadow
Minister for Racing, The Hon. Tim Bull, who care
greatly about the future of Victorian racing and
continue to be great advocates for the industry.
The Government’s support through the Victorian
Racing Industry Fund (VRIF) and Major Racing
Events Fund (MREF) helps key projects and
initiatives around major events, horse and participant
welfare, tracks and racing facilities become reality
and for that we are immensely grateful.
MREF helped grow attendances at race meetings
across the state and allowed clubs to better
showcase our world class racing to more Victorians
and visitors. This in turn helped the 2024 Spring
Racing Carnival generate $785 million in economic
benefit for Victoria.
I’d like to take this opportunity to thank my fellow
Board members, Aaron and his Executive Team, and
all the staff across the RV Group for their outstanding
efforts and contributions over the past 12 months.
I’d also like to acknowledge all of our metropolitan
and country clubs who once again did an amazing
job putting on the show and uniting communities
with great hospitality and entertainment to
complement the racing action.
Finally, I’d like to thank all the owners, trainers,
jockeys and punters who have supported Victorian
racing throughout the period. Whilst there is often
a spotlight on what happens off the track, it is what
happens on it that will always be the magic that
captivates us all and drives our industry forward.
Your sincerely,
Tim Eddy

CEO’s Report
We made important and significant changes to the
corporate governance structure of our owned media
business, Racing.com Media (RDCM), which came
under RV operational management from 1 January
2025, to better align these assets with our broader
industry growth strategy and to realise important
operational efficiencies.
With that strategic restructure now complete, a major
focus of our media arm has been to consolidate the
future of broadcasting for the sport, and advocate
and promote the VTRI to the widest possible
audience. I am delighted to report that we have made
giant strides in that particular space, securing new
long-term broadcast agreements with the Seven
Network (free-to-air and digital), Foxtel and Kayo
whilst also welcoming Western Australian racing
into the RDCM fold, thus growing its audience.
The revamp of the media business continues into
FY26 with the planned launch of the enhanced
Racing.com website, the digital migration of
vital industry publication Inside Racing, and the
finalisation of the sale of industry-owned audio
broadcast business and radio network, RSN Racing
& Sports (RSN) to Sports Entertainment Network
(SEN). Further, we also extended a range of major
commercial media partnerships to ensure VTR is the
most widely distributed racing product that reaches
the biggest audiences, positioning the industry for
further growth.
The ongoing growth in engagement post the COVID
era has also been reflected in attendance figures,
with total attendances at Victorian race meetings
increasing by almost 5% in FY25 to 1.16 million. This
was underlined by excellent crowd growth across all
Spring Racing Carnival meetings, with the four days
at Flemington over Melbourne Cup Week the highest
attended race meetings in Australia in FY25, as
attendances across the week grew 9% year-on-year.
It was pleasing to see this upward trend spread
beyond metropolitan areas, with total attendance
at country meetings up 10% from FY24 to FY25.
Turnover was consistent with FY24 across the
Country Cup meetings run during the Spring Racing
Carnival, defying the broader first half wagering trends
and underscoring the importance of country racing.
The introduction of Racing Forums in FY25
contributed markedly to our goal of increasing
collaboration with industry stakeholders and clubs.
They provided a platform for industry leaders to
share ideas and work together methodically on key
decisions and initiatives around our racing program.
RV’s unwavering commitment to industry and
participant support saw expenditure in this area
increase by 13.9% to $35.286 million in FY25,
with new workforce development programs and
initiatives like mental health first aid workshops
helping to ensure the highest standards of welfare
for our participants and thoroughbreds.
Equine welfare continues to be one of the key pillars
of our industry, and RV’s $5.94 million investment
into equine welfare through expenditure and capital
investment across FY25 took our total investment
since the 2017 introduction of a contribution from
prizemoney to $44 million.
We have been focused on providing greater
transparency on how the 2% contribution from
prizemoney is being allocated to help improve equine
welfare outcomes and maintain our social licence
with several industry updates throughout FY25.
On the track, our veterinary protocols continue
to set new global safety standards in FY25 as we
celebrated the fourth consecutive Spring Racing
Carnival without serious injury, whilst our Off The
Track program continues to assist in the transition
of thoroughbreds to life after racing by creating
demand and promoting the unique versatility of
the thoroughbred.
Our emergency aid program was the source of a
key investment in FY25 with $800,000 in drought
relief delivered to support more than 1,800 eligible
thoroughbreds in the face of challenging conditions
throughout country Victoria.
As we look ahead to FY26, our focus will be on
a return to break-even whilst maintaining vital
returns and support for participants and owners.
Our restructure and efficiency program throughout
FY25 provides the foundation to achieve that
objective, whilst we focus on engagement and
support from punters for a great racing program.
At this juncture, I would like to extend my deepest
gratitude to every industry participant and owner,
who, along with our equine athletes, form the
lifeblood of our great industry. I’d also like to thank
all our metropolitan and country clubs for the pivotal
role they play in showcasing our sport and engaging
with the community – this is essential for the
continued prosperity of the VTRI.
Finally, my thanks also go to the RV Board, led by
Chair Tim Eddy, for their continued support and
leadership; and to my Executive team and all RV staff
members for their incredible contribution to ensuring
the success and viability of VTR for the benefit of all.
Yours Sincerely,
Aaron Morrison
Chief Executive Officer
It is with great pleasure that I present the
performance of RV for FY25, together with
an overview of the key initiatives and activities
undertaken to maintain and strengthen Victoria’s
position as Australia’s and one of the world’s leading
thoroughbred racing jurisdictions.
In my first full year as Chief Executive, our key focus
has been on improving promotion of the sport and
engagement with a broader audience, striving for
greater transparency and collaboration with industry
stakeholders, ensuring our business model is lean
and efficient, and maximising returns to participants
and those who invest in our sport. Whilst positive
progress has been made on all of those fronts, there
is still work to be done, and efforts to strengthen
outcomes around those key strategic priorities will
continue in FY26 and beyond.
In FY25, RV recorded a normalised net deficit for
underlying business performance of $2.532 million,
which was slightly better than budgeted. This comes
after adjustments to exclude one-off accounting
and non-business-as-usual activity. Our statutory
reported net deficit was $8.885 million, which is
an improvement on the $11.834 million reported
net deficit in FY24 that came after a run of 1 1
consecutive surpluses.
Whilst macroeconomic headwinds and the transition
of long-term industry funding arrangements were
expected to place downward pressure on key
revenue streams this year, maintaining strong returns
to owners, participants and clubs and continuing to
invest in world-class racing and training infrastructure
has always remained a clear priority, and we are
pleased to have been able to deliver on this.
To support this objective, RV implemented a
business-wide efficiency program spanning the
2024 and 2025 reporting periods, which has
delivered ongoing savings - a cumulative reduction
in operational expenditure of $13.484 million over the
two years. Decisions taken in the second half of FY25
are also expected to generate further annualised
efficiencies in FY26 and beyond as we right-size our
business and work towards returning to break-even
whilst continuing to invest in growth.
Whilst the level of engagement in Victorian
Thoroughbred Racing (VTR) remained strong, a range
of factors led to a small decrease in the amount invested
by active customers with wagering turnover declining
by 4.0% to $7.611 billion. This total is 8.4% above
pre-COVID levels and remains at nation-leading levels
amongst Australian racing codes. With the increased
PoCT rate coupled with regulatory uncertainty
around Federal Government reforms, FY25 saw
decreased investment in both customer marketing and
promotions, compounding broader macroeconomic
pressures impacting the wagering market.
Improving macro conditions and increased investment
into our own content-led promotion saw wagering
turnover on VTR bounce back in the second half of
FY25, partially mitigating the wagering conditions
experienced in the first half. I am pleased to report
that early wagering figures obtained from the opening
quarter of FY26 indicate that trend of consolidation
and the positive momentum has continued.
FY25 saw a transition from the historic VicTAB
Joint Venture (JV) arrangements to a new multi-
year industry distribution model agreed between the
State Government and the Victorian Racing Industry
(VRI) which provides greater exposure to future
growth in the wagering market sector with long-term
certainty for racing in Victoria. Noting this substantial
transition, combined revenue from wagering and
government sources declined marginally by 1.1% to
$421.821 million, representing an outperformance
relative to our wagering turnover.

Racing & Programming
New Initiatives
• 	The All-Star Mile (1600m) returned to
Flemington in 2025 and carried Group 1 status
for the first time, having consistently exceeded
the benchmark for elite level recognition since
its inception in 2019. The race also formed part
of Super Saturday for the first time, joining the
time-honoured Group 1 Newmarket Handicap
on a card that now showcases the very best
of Australia’s sprinters and milers. The All-Star
Mile’s new position on the calendar strengthens
its link with the Group 1 Australian Cup (2000m)
at the same venue three weeks later, while
Tom Kitten’s short head defeat of fan favourite
Mr Brightside ensured the race again rated
comfortably inside the top 10 races of the
2024-25 Victorian racing season. Changes were
also made to the All-Star Mile entry conditions
in FY25, which now comprise nominations,
‘win-and-you’re-in’ races and wildcards.
• 	The Moir Stakes (1000m) and Manikato Stakes
(1200m) at The Valley were moved earlier in
the spring in FY25, with the aim of providing
an enhanced Group 1 pathway for Australia’s
best sprinters and better aligning with the
national program. Punters responded well to
the changes, with wagering turnover on the
2024 Moir up a hefty 44.9% year-on-year.
The Manikato, meanwhile, returning to its
position on AFL Grand Final Eve for the first
time since 2011, delivered one of the most
spectacular finishes of the season, with
syndicated horse Southport Tycoon securing
his future at stud with a last gasp victory over
his stablemate Growing Empire. On a landmark
night under the lights for the Ciaron Maher
juggernaut, Pride Of Jenni had The Valley
rocking with her all-the-way victory in the
Feehan Stakes (1600m), which was also staged
on Grand Final Eve for the first time having
previously been run in early September.
• 	FY25 saw new ground broken in Victoria with
the introduction of the state’s first ever slot
races. The inaugural running of The Meteorite
(1200m), held on Cranbourne Cup Day in
November, was won by emerging sprinter Nadal,
while The Supernova (1400m) run four weeks
later in December on the relocated Pakenham
Cup Day card, was taken out by Here To Shock
on his way to Group 1 success at his next start.
Both slot races, each carrying a $1 million purse,
generated strong engagement and were the
highest turnover races on the day.
• 	To complement the successful Sportsbet Future
Stars Series held in the state’s south-east in
January to March, a new $1 million Emerging
Stars maiden series was launched over the other
side of town in FY25. Funded by prizemoney
re-directed from the All-Star Mile, the lucrative
series - which is open to three, four and five-
year-old maiden gallopers - comprised 10
heats worth $75,000 each across Ballarat,
Warrnambool, and Geelong. The heats were
conducted over 1200m to 1400m between
October and December and culminated in the
Sportsbet Emerging Stars Final (1400m) on
Ballarat Cup Day, with the inaugural winner
Just For Show going on to land the VOBIS
Gold Carat (1200m) at his next start.
• 	Another key race date initiative in
FY25 was the trial of Saturday night
meetings at Cranbourne over four
consecutive weeks in late December
and January, replacing what was
previously a Friday night meeting.
On-course attendance was particularly
strong, with almost 10,000 patrons
enjoying the action across the period,
up 28% on the corresponding Friday night
meetings in the previous season. Wagering
turnover also performed well for the timeslot
with an average over $9 million per meeting.
• 	FY25 saw the commencement of RV’s Racing
Forums, which invite all stakeholder groups to
provide views on a number of racing related
topics such as race programs, race dates,
handicapping and VOBIS. The Racing Forum
was convened three times in FY25 and played
a crucial role in the restructuring of the 2025
Spring Racing Carnival, with the Group 1 Sir
Rupert Clarke Stakes (1400m) returning to its
original position in the program in September,
the Group 1 Thousand Guineas (1600m) moving
back into Caulfield Cup week and the Group 1
C.F. Orr Stakes (1400m) shifting from February
to Caulfield’s meeting on Saturday 15 November.
The weight scales for the 2025 Caulfield and
Melbourne Cups were also altered as a result
of consultation through the Racing Forums,
with the minimum allotted weight for both
Group 1 races increased from 50kg to 51kg
and the minimum topweight raised from
58kg to 59kg. A minimum topweight of 57kg
at final acceptance time was also reinstated
for both feature events.
Additional Program Updates
• 	Following consultation with stakeholders
and a review of the 10-month trial between
August 2023 and May 2024, 10-race Saturday
metropolitan meetings were made permanent
in FY25 for a reduced period of eight months
each season. The new schedule began with
the Group 1 Memsie Stakes meeting on
31 August 2024 and ended with the meeting
at Mornington on 19 April 2025.
• 	Having been held at Yarra Valley in 2024,
the Country Discovery Raceday continued
its voyage around regional Victoria and was
staged at Echuca in April 2025. Headlined by
the $500,000 Country Discovery (1000m),
which holds the coveted title of regional
Victoria’s richest sprint race, it was speed
machine Midwest who landed the Good Friday
spoils this year under a power-packed front-
running ride from Craig Williams. Ballarat will
play host to the fifth annual Country Discovery
Raceday on 3 April 2026.
• 	RV, MRC and Thoroughbred Breeders Victoria
(TBV) came together to deliver the richest
ever edition of Victorian Owners and Breeders
Raceday (VOBRD), which moved to a prime
position on the calendar during the Festival
of Racing in March 2025.

Owners & Breeders
In FY25, a total of 64,706 people experienced the thrill
of owning a racehorse in Victoria, which represents
a slight decrease on the FY24 figure of 65,407.
Notwithstanding the fractional decline, Victoria still
boasts more registered owners, part-owners and owners
aged 25 or younger than any other racing jurisdiction in
Australia, while registered syndicate members are almost
double that of the next placed state.
Victorian Owners Gold Card (VOGC)
The VOGC program continues to reward owners
who support the state’s racing industry by granting
them a range of benefits and privileges at all
Victorian race clubs.
Work continued throughout FY25 on the

Safety Record
Victoria’s raceday fatality rate for flat racing remains
among the lowest in the world of thoroughbred
racing. Reducing that rate towards 0.00% is an
ongoing focus of RV’s investment in veterinary
and welfare initiatives.
In the 2024-25 racing season, there were 20 fatalities
in flat racing in Victoria from 41,510 starts. This is
equivalent to a fatality rate of 0.048%, which is down
on the 0.055% fatality rate in the 2023-24 season.
Equally, efforts to reduce the rate of incidents in jumps
racing continue and a number of key changes were
introduced following the 2024 Jumps Racing Review.
In the 2025 jumps racing season, which ran from late
March until the middle of August, there were three
fatalities in jumps races in Victoria from 405 starters.
That is equivalent to a fatality rate of 0.74%, down
from 1.2% in the 2023-24 season.
Veterinary Protocols
RV’s world first veterinary protocols were again
in place for the 2024 Spring Racing Carnival and
delivered a fourth consecutive Melbourne Cup
without a serious injury.
Following a period of extensive consultation with
both local and international participants, in FY25
RV announced enhanced processes for the 2025
Spring Racing Carnival veterinary protocols, with the
aim of improving transparency and communication
with participants.
For international horses competing in Victoria

2024 Jumps Racing Review
Following an extensive Jumps Racing Review,
RV confirmed on 13 December 2024 that jumps
racing will continue in Victoria, supported by
significant safety and operational enhancements.
The review, led by the Jumps Review Group (JRG)
chaired by RV’s Executive General Manager of
Integrity, Jamie Stier, and supported by former
Supreme Court Judge, Justice Jack Forrest,
examined the sport’s viability and safety. Over 350
public submissions were received, reflecting diverse
views ranging from improvement proposals to calls
for discontinuation.
After considering the JRG’s findings and
recommendations, the RV Board endorsed
the continuation of jumps racing and committed
to implementing 11
key recommendations
designed to strengthen
safety, governance,
and operational standards.
The endorsed measures included
shortening the length of the
jumps racing season, strengthening
track compliance and preparation
standards, enhancing trial and schooling
opportunities, and introducing a dedicated
Jumps Jockey Coach. The number of jumps
races programmed was reduced from 65 to 57
in 2025, while changes were made to improve
the financial sustainability of the code. To ensure
accountability, a new Jumps Taskforce was
created to oversee the implementation of these
recommendations, monitor safety and performance
outcomes, and report annually to the RV Board.

Removal of Serious Offences
from the Rules of Racing of RV
In May 2025, the RV Board approved the removal
of several Australian Rules from the definition of
“Serious Offence” under the Rules of Racing of RV,
effective 1 July 2025. This change was endorsed by
the Victorian Racing Integrity Board and recognises
that, in many cases, it is more beneficial for both
participants and the RV Integrity Team to enable
the Stewards to hear charges in the first instance
while maintaining the right for participants to appeal
any finding of the Stewards to the Victorian Racing
Tribunal (VRT). This change should also deliver a
decrease in time taken to conclude the hearing of
charges under these Rules.
Other Notable Amendments
to the Rules of Racing of RV
AR 55A Frequency of Race Starts
Effective from 1 May 2025, AR 55A was amended to
prohibit a horse from starting in more than one race
per day, to allow consecutive day starts only with
approval from the Stewards or RV, and to restrict
a horse to no more than five race starts within any
30-day period. Stewards retain discretion to impose
further limitations where necessary to protect
horse welfare.
AR283(6)(e) Minimum Mandatory Penalties for
Electrical Apparatus Offences
Effective from 1 May 2025, AR 283(6)(e) was
amended to increase the mandatory minimum
penalty for breaches of AR 231 which relates to
the care and welfare of horses. The amendment saw
the minimum penalty for the use, or attempted use,
of any electric or electronic apparatus increase from
two years to five years.

Wagering
Due to a combination of macroeconomic and
regulatory factors, wagering turnover on VTR
fell by 4.0% year-on-year in FY25.
In addition, wagering service providers (WSPs) were
required to absorb the cost of a 50% increase in the
Point of Consumption Tax (PoCT), which rose from
10% to 15% on 1 July 2024. WSPs responded to this
PoCT increase by offering less competitive betting
markets and by decreasing their spend on customer
generosities (free bet turnover was down by 6%
year-on-year), both of which had a negative impact
on turnover.
Despite these headwinds, data provided by WSPs
shows that VTR attracted turnover from more than
1.3 million digital customers in FY25 and continues
to see customer growth. Turnover remains 8.4%
above FY19, which was the final year prior
to COVID lockdowns.

revenue streams to support that expenditure and
provide opportunities to promote the VTRI across
multiple consumer segments.

Revenue
The total revenue from operations decreased by -1.9% to $526.501 million versus the prior year (2024:
$536.755 million).
Total wagering revenue for the full year decreased by $129.277 million (-35.5%) versus the prior year as the
industry funding model transitioned away from the VICTAB Joint Venture (JV) which ceased on 15 August
2024. This largely reflects a re-classification of how we report the source of income post the end of the JV,
and the commencement of the new State Government industry distribution arrangements from July 2024.
Further details on the transition are provided both below and within Note 1(iii) of the financial statements.
Total wagering turnover of $7.611 billion (2024: $7.930 billion) represented a -4.0% decline on the prior year.
Whilst there is acknowledgement around the intrinsic link between wagering and racing, market uncertainty
regarding the Federal Government’s response to the findings of the Parliamentary Inquiry into Online
Gambling Harm continues to influence wagering service providers (WSP) investment. At a macroeconomic
level, the weakest year-on-year GDP growth recorded since the early 1990’s (+1.3%) impacted consumer
spending and by extension, expenditure on discretionary goods and services such as wagering.
The year-on-year decline in turnover coincided with the increase in the Victorian Point of Consumption Tax
(PoCT) from 10% to 15% on 1 July 2024, the cost of which is absorbed by WSPs. Along with an increase
in other taxes, product fees and regulatory costs nationally and combined with tougher macroeconomic
conditions, WSPs have reacted in the form of less competitive odds pricing (higher overrounds), and
decreased investment in customer marketing, promotions and generosities.
The industry funding framework shift, following the execution of a new $1.17 billion ($864 million indicative
value) twenty-year Wagering and Betting Licence between Tabcorp Holdings Limited and the Victorian
Government, resulted in a transition away from the previous JV to a new funding arrangement, driven by
the industry generated PoCT and fixed distribution from the State Government. This source increased by
$124.449 million versus the prior year.
From 1 July 2024, the State Government increased the PoCT levied on wagering by Victorian residents, with
the rate of PoCT collected increasing from 10% to 15% of Net Wagering Revenue (NWR). The Victorian Racing
Industry (VRI) distribution payment agreed to be paid to the VRI by the State Government increased from
3.5% to 7.5% of NWR effective from the same date. In line with the transition to the new VRI framework PoCT
distributions to the consolidated entity increased by $67.278 million versus the prior year.
On 30 August 2024, the VRI together with the State Government executed the VRI Funding Deed. This
ten-year agreement, which was backdated to commence on 16 August 2024, provides a transition of industry
funding to the VRI.
The thoroughbred, harness and greyhound racing codes have agreed on an appropriate code sharing
arrangement of the above VRI distributions.
Victorian Racing Industry Funding (VRIF) grants were also received to fund infrastructure projects during
the period and to support initiatives including major events, owners ticketing, equine welfare limb injury
prevention and the VOBIS program.
Media revenue decreased by $1.324 million (-1.5%) to $85.372 million. The decline primarily related to a
softening in the wagering advertising market and impacted revenue generated by Racing.com.
Funds under investment (excluding withdrawals) increased in value over the period due to continued strong
interest income and growth in major financial markets. Total investment revenue, including realised gains/
losses, unrealised gains/losses and investment income in the form of dividends and interest was positive for
the year at +$6.359 million (2024: +$9.091 million).

Industry Expenditure
Industry expenditure reflects targeted funding and investment provided to owners, participants and clubs
and accounts for the majority of the consolidated entity expenditure (75% of revenue).
• 	Whilst total Prizemoney and VOBIS bonuses on offer remained in line with prior year at circa
$316 million, Racing Victoria’s contribution towards this expenditure slightly decreased by $3.306
million (-1.2%) to $265.761 million, primarily due to an allocation of prizemoney now being directed
towards the welfare of participants, combined with the variable nature of items such as bonus strike
rates, field sizes, divided races and meeting abandonments. An increase in club and other partner
contributions towards prizemoney enabled the ‘on offer’ amount to remain in line with prior year.
• 	Club funding expenditure increased by $1.681 million (+1.9%) to $89.953 million. The annual increase
is largely due a revision in the composition of some club funding arrangements. Club funding is
undertaken in accordance with funding arrangements in place with each club, which have both fixed
and performance-based components.
• 	Infrastructure expenditure decreased by $5.681 million (-52.6%) to $5.126 million. This reflects the
completion of major infrastructure projects.
• 	Industry and participant support increased by $4.306 million (+13.9%) to $35.286 million. This spend
reflects the continued commitment to support the industry through various initiatives and programs,
most notably those supporting the welfare of our participants and thoroughbreds. Other forms
of support include jockey ride fees, jockey insurance coverage, workforce development including
the apprentice program and owner initiatives. The increase on prior year primarily relates to the
spend tied to the additional 1% of prizemoney funding from 1 February 2024 being directed towards
supporting participants, including commitments to support rising trainer Workcover premiums.
Operational Expenditure (incl. Operating, Media & Wagering Expenditure)
Total operational expenditure decreased by $4.875 million (-3.6%) to $128.718 million.
The decline in operational expenditure extends further following the exclusion of some key non-BAU activity
in the reporting year, primarily incurred to unlock future strategic benefit to the VTRI. Following adjustment,
normalised operational expenditure for the reporting year reflects a decrease of $6.621 million (-5.1%) on the prior
year, with the full benefit of some of these reductions to be realised on an annualised basis in future periods.
Operating expenditure includes the core costs of managing and administering the VTRI, including:
• 	racing operations (including race day services, vets and quarantine);
• 	integrity and stewarding;
• 	media and wagering;
• 	marketing, public relations and communications; and
• 	executive, strategy, administration, technology, legal, risk and compliance.
Highlighting operational expenditure separate from other expenditure activities allows for better external
benchmarking and an assessment of the efficiency of the stewardship of Victorian thoroughbred racing
operations. Management continues to pursue cost control and process efficiency improvements across all
key activities.
Depreciation, Amortisation and Other Asset Expenditure
Depreciation, amortisation and other asset expenditure decreased by $6.612 million (-43.3%) to $8.654
million. This expenditure decrease relates to the prior year loss on disposal associated with the below market
value transfer of Pakenham stabling allotments and Cranbourne land to the Cranbourne Pakenham Racing
Club as part of the agreed combination arrangements between Racing Victoria and the merged Club’s.
Investment Activities
Racing Victoria holds and invests financial assets on behalf of the VTRI as part of an Industry Sustainability
Fund (ISF). The purpose of the ISF is to ensure that the industry retains sufficient funds to protect the long-
term sustainability of the industry, support the operating financial position and maintenance of important
industry support during times of operational uncertainty, provide funds to support important industry-wide
capital infrastructure investments and to assist with finance and investment that may not be otherwise
available.
The ISF framework defines its assets as those investments which have a broader industry benefit with an
investment or commercial return objective, interest bearing loans with related parties (at discount to market
rates), and land earmarked for development with a commercial return.
The consolidated entity engages Evans and Partners to manage and invest accumulated surplus cash that
sits outside operating cash requirements. A balanced investment mandate is maintained, with investments
made across various asset classes including cash, interest rate securities, Australian and international equities,
property, infrastructure and alternative investments. The investment mandate and asset allocations are
reviewed on an annual basis together with the governing investment policy.
At 30 June 2025 these financial assets totalled $57.839 million (2024: $72.956 million).
The movement from prior year can largely be attributed to drawdowns to manage the consolidated entity’s
net deficit and negative operating cash flow through the reporting period.

24. Material Accounting Policies
The following significant accounting policies have been adopted in the preparation and presentation of the
financial report:
(a) 	Borrowings and Borrowing Costs
Borrowings are recorded initially at fair value, net of transaction costs. Subsequent to initial
recognition, borrowings are measured at amortised cost with any difference between the initial
recognised amount and the redemption value being recognised in profit and loss over the period of
the borrowing using the effective interest rate method.
Other borrowing costs are recognised as an expense in the period in which they are incurred.
(b) 	Cash and Cash Equivalents
Cash comprises cash on hand and demand deposits. Cash equivalents are short term, highly liquid
investments that are readily convertible to known amounts of cash and are subject to an insignificant
risk of changes in value.
(c) 	Employee Benefits
A liability is recognised for benefits accruing to employees in respect of wages and salaries, annual
leave, long service leave, and sick leave when it is probable that settlement will be required, and they
are capable of being measured reliably.
Liabilities recognised in respect of employee benefits expected to be settled within 12 months are
measured at their nominal values, using the remuneration rate expected to apply at the time of
settlement.
Liabilities recognised in respect of employee benefits which are not expected to be settled within 12
months are measured as the present value of the estimated future cash outflows to be made by the
consolidated entity in respect of services provided by employees up to the reporting date.
(d) 	Financial assets
Classification and measurement of financial assets
All recognised financial assets that are within the scope of AASB 9 are required to be subsequently
measured at amortised cost or fair value on the basis of the entity’s business model for managing the
financial assets and the contractual cash flow characteristics of the financial assets. Specifically:
• 	debt investments that are held within a business model whose objective is to collect the contractual
cash flows, and that have contractual cash flows that are solely payments of principal and interest on
the principal amount outstanding, are subsequently measured at amortised cost;
• 	all other debt investments and equity investments are subsequently measured at fair value through
profit and loss (FVTPL).
Despite the foregoing, the consolidated entity may make the following irrevocable election/
designation at initial recognition of a financial asset:
• 	the consolidated entity may irrevocably elect to present subsequent changes in fair value of an equity
investment that is neither held for trading nor contingent consideration recognised by an acquirer in
a business combination to which IFRS 3 applies in other comprehensive income; and
• 	the consolidated entity may irrevocably designate a debt investment that meets the amortised cost
or fair value through other comprehensive income (FVTOCI) criteria as measured at FVTPL if doing
so eliminates or significantly reduces an accounting mismatch.
Debt instruments that are subsequently measured at amortised cost or at FVTOCI are subject to
impairment testing.
Impairment of financial assets
In relation to the impairment of financial assets, AASB 9 requires an expected credit loss (ECL) model
as opposed to an incurred credit loss model under IAS 39. The ECL model requires the consolidated
entity to account for expected credit losses and changes in those expected credit losses at each
reporting date to reflect changes in credit risk since initial recognition of the financial assets. In other
words, it is no longer necessary for a credit event to have occurred before credit losses are recognised.
The consolidated entity reviewed its related party loan receivables for impairment under the General
Approach to determine whether there has been a Significant Increase in Credit Risk (SICR) since initial
recognition and whether loans are credit impaired. This determines whether the loans are in Stage 1,
Stage 2, or Stage 3, which in turn determines both;
• 	the amount of ECL to be recognised: 12-month ECL or Lifetime ECL; and
• 	the amount of interest income to be recognised in future reporting periods: Effective Interest Rate
(EIR) based on gross carrying amount of the loan which excludes ECL or the net carrying amount (i.e.
the amortised cost) which includes ECL.
Lifetime ECL are the ECL that result from all possible default events over the expected life of loans
whereas 12-month ECL are a portion of Lifetime ECL that represent the ECL that result from default
events that are possible within 12 months of the reporting date.
The consolidated entity reviewed its related party loans and assessed that none of the loans have
suffered SICR with no further credit losses recognised within 12 months of the reporting date.
Loss allowances for trade receivables are measured at an amount equal to lifetime ECLs. When
determining whether the credit risk of a financial asset has increased significantly since initial
recognition and when estimating ECLs, the consolidated entity considers reasonable and supportable
information that is relevant and available without undue cost or effort. This is determined by reference
to past default experience.

(g) 	Impairment of Other Tangible and Intangible Assets
At each reporting date, the consolidated entity reviews the carrying amounts of its tangible and
intangible assets to determine whether there is any indication that those assets have suffered an
impairment loss. If any such indication exists, the recoverable amount of the asset is estimated to
determine the extent of the impairment loss (if any). Where the asset does not generate cash flows
that are independent from other assets, the consolidated entity estimates the recoverable amount of
the cash generating unit to which the asset belongs.
Where a reasonable and consistent basis can be identified, corporate assets are also allocated to
individual cash generating units, otherwise they are allocated the smallest group of cash generating
units for which a reasonable and consistent allocation basis can be identified.
Intangible assets with indefinite useful lives and intangible assets not yet available for use are tested
for impairment annually and whenever there is an indication that the asset may be impaired.
Recoverable amount is the higher of fair value less costs to sell and value in use. In assessing value in
use, the estimated future cash flows are discounted to their present value using a post-tax discount
rate that reflects current market assessments of the time value of money and the risks specific to the
asset for which the estimates of future cash flows have not been adjusted. Notwithstanding this, value
in use is depreciated replacement cost of an asset when the future economic benefits of the asset are
not primarily dependent on the asset’s ability to generate net cash inflows and where the consolidated
entity would, if deprived of the asset, replace its remaining future economic benefits.
If the recoverable amount of an asset (or cash-generating unit) is estimated to be less than it’s carrying
amount, the carrying amount of the asset (cash-generating unit) is reduced to its recoverable amount.
An impairment loss is recognised in profit or loss immediately, unless the relevant asset is carried at
fair value, in which case the impairment loss is treated as a revaluation decrease.
Where an impairment loss subsequently reverses, the carrying amount of the asset (cash-generating
unit) is increased to the revised estimate of its recoverable amount, but only to the extent that the
increased carrying amount does not exceed the carrying amount that would have been determined
had no impairment loss been recognised for the asset (cash-generating unit) in prior periods.
A reversal of an impairment loss is recognised in profit or loss immediately, unless the relevant asset
is carried at fair value, in which case the reversal of the impairment loss is treated as a revaluation
increase.

The Group recognises a right-of-use asset and a lease liability at the lease commencement date. The
right-of-use asset is initially measured at cost, which comprises the initial amount of the lease liability
adjusted for any lease payments made at or before the commencement date, plus any initial direct
costs incurred and an estimate of costs to dismantle and remove the underlying asset or to restore the
underlying asset or the site on which it is located, less any lease incentives received.
The right-of-use asset is subsequently depreciated using the straight-line method from the
commencement date to the end of the lease term, unless the lease transfers ownership of the
underlying asset to the Group by the end of the lease term or the cost of the right-of-use asset reflects
that the Group will exercise a purchase option. In that case the right-of-use asset will be depreciated
over the useful life of the underlying asset, which is determined on the same basis as those of property
and equipment. In addition, the right-of-use asset is periodically reduced by impairment losses, if any,
and adjusted for certain remeasurements of the lease liability. The lease liability is initially measured
at the present value of the lease payments that are not paid at the commencement date, discounted
using the interest rate implicit in the lease or, if that rate cannot be readily determined, the Group’s
incremental borrowing rate. Generally, the Group uses its incremental borrowing rate as the discount
rate.
The Group determines its incremental borrowing rate by obtaining interest rates from various external
financing sources and makes certain adjustments to reflect the terms of the lease and type of the asset
leased. Lease payments included in the measurement of the lease liability comprise the following:
• 	fixed payments, including in-substance fixed payments;
• 	variable lease payments that depend on an index or a rate, initially measured using the index or rate a
at the commencement date;
• 	amounts expected to be payable under a residual value guarantee; and
• 	the exercise price under a purchase option that the Group is reasonably certain to exercise, lease
payments in an optional renewal period if the Group is reasonably certain to exercise an extension
option, and penalties for early termination of a lease unless the Group is reasonably certain not to
terminate early.
The lease liability is measured at amortised cost using the effective interest method. It is remeasured
when there is a change in future lease payments arising from a change in an index or rate, if there
is a change in the Group’s estimate of the amount expected to be payable under a residual value
guarantee, if the Group changes its assessment of whether it will exercise a purchase, extension or
termination option or if there is a revised in-substance fixed lease payment.
When the lease liability is remeasured in this way, a corresponding adjustment is made to the carrying
amount of the right-of-use asset, or is recorded in profit or loss if the carrying amount of the right-of-
use asset has been reduced to zero.

service providers for the publication and use of Victorian thoroughbred race fields.
Rendering of Services
Revenue from a contract to provide services is recognised by reference to the stage of completion of
the contract. The stage of the contract is determined as follows:
• 	installation fees are recognised by reference to the stage of completion of the installation, determined
as the proportion of the total time expected to install that has elapsed at reporting date;
• 	servicing fees included in the price of products sold are recognised by reference to the proportion
of the total cost of providing the servicing for the product sold, considering historical trends in the
number of services actually provided on past goods sold; and
• 	revenue from time and material contracts is recognised at the contractual rates as labour hours are
delivered and direct expenses incurred.
Contributions of Assets
Revenue arising from the contribution of assets is recognised when the consolidated entity gains
control of the contribution or the right to receive the contribution.
Interest revenue
Interest revenue is accrued on a time basis, by reference to the principal outstanding and effective
interest rate applicable, which is the rate that exactly discounts estimated future cash receipts through
the expected life of the financial asset to the assets carrying amount.
Government Grants
Government grants are not recognised until there is reasonable assurance that the consolidated entity
will comply with the conditions attaching to them and that the grants will be received.
Government grants are recognised in profit or loss on a systematic basis over the periods in which
the consolidated entity recognises as expenses the related costs for which the grants are intended
to compensate. Specifically, Government grants whose primary condition is that the consolidated
entity should purchase, construct or otherwise acquire non-current assets are recognised as deferred
revenue in the consolidated statement of financial position and transferred to profit or loss on a
systematic and rational basis over the useful lives of the related assets.
Government grants that are receivable as compensation for expenses or losses already incurred or for
the purpose of giving immediate financial support to the consolidated entity with no future related
costs are recognised in profit or loss in the period in which they become receivable.
VOBIS Nominations
VOBIS nomination fees received are deferred into unearned revenue, to be recognised as revenue
in future years based upon the performance obligation of VOBIS bonuses on offer to eligible horses
dependant on age.

(o) 	Investments in Associates
An associate is an entity over which the consolidated entity has significant influence. Significant
influence is the power to participate in the financial and operating policy decisions of the investee but
is not control or joint control over those policies.
The results and assets and liabilities of associates are incorporated in these consolidated financial
statements using the equity method of accounting, except when the investment, or a portion thereof,
is classified as held for sale, in which case it is accounted for in accordance with AASB 5. Under the
equity method, an investment in an associate is initially recognised in the consolidated statement of
financial position at cost and adjusted thereafter to recognise the Group’s share of the profit or loss
and other comprehensive income of the associate.

(p) 	Subsidiaries

for the period then ended. Racing Victoria and its subsidiaries together are referred to in this financial

Subsidiaries are all entities (including special purpose entities) over which the consolidated entity has
control. Control is achieved when the Company:
• 	has power over the investee;
• 	is exposed, or has rights, to variable returns from its involvement with the investee; and
• 	has the ability to use its power to affect its returns.
The Company reassesses whether or not it controls an investee if facts and circumstances indicate that
there are changes to one or more of the three elements of control listed above. When the Company
has less than a majority of the voting rights of an investee, it has power over the investee when the
voting rights are sufficient to give it the practical ability to direct the relevant activities of the investee
unilaterally. The Company considers all relevant facts and circumstances in assessing whether or not
the Company’s voting rights in an investee are sufficient to give it power, including:
• 	the size of the Company’s holding of voting rights relative to the size and dispersion of holdings of
the other vote holders;
• 	potential voting rights held by the Company, other vote holders or other parties;
• 	rights arising from other contractual arrangements; and
• 	any additional facts and circumstances that indicate that the Company has, or does not have, the
current ability to direct the relevant activities at the time that decisions need to be made, including
voting patterns at previous shareholders’ meetings.
Consolidation of a subsidiary begins when the Company obtains control over the subsidiary and
ceases when the Company loses control of the subsidiary. Specifically, income and expenses of a
subsidiary acquired or disposed of during the year are included in the consolidated statement of profit

(r) 	Goodwill
Goodwill arising on an acquisition of a business is carried at cost as established at the date of the
acquisition of the business less accumulated impairment losses, if any.
For the purposes of impairment testing, goodwill is allocated to each of the Group’s cash-generating
units (or groups of cash-generating units) that is expected to benefit from the synergies of the
combination.
A cash-generating unit to which goodwill has been allocated is tested for impairment annually, or
more frequently when there is an indication that the unit may be impaired. If the recoverable amount
of the cash-generating unit is less than its carrying amount, the impairment loss is allocated first to
reduce the carrying amount of any goodwill allocated to the unit and then to the other assets of the
unit pro rata based on the carrying amount of each asset in the unit. Any impairment loss for goodwill
is recognised directly in profit or loss. An impairment loss recognised for goodwill is not reversed in
subsequent periods.
On disposal of the relevant cash-generating unit, the attributable amount of goodwill is included in the
determination of the profit or loss on disposal.
