Automating the Annual Report

A production plan for FY26, built from a review of the FY24 and FY25 reports.

1   What we are solving

You named two pain points: InDesign turnaround and cost, and proofing and fact-checking. This plan is aimed at those two and nothing else.

The information-gathering that Comms co-ordinates across departments stays a human job. What changes is what happens to the information after it arrives. Today it is retyped — from an Excel file into an InDesign table, from a draft into a layout, from a chart into a redrawn graphic. Every retype is a design task and a proofing task at the same time. Removing the retype removes both.

2   What the last two reports show

The artefact is stable. Both years run the same three-part spine: front editorial (Chair, CEO, Key Performance Indicators and ten themed sections), then the Directors' Report and audit material, then the financial statements and notes. FY24 ran 45 pages, FY25 ran 43.

The design effort is concentrated. In FY25, pages 4 to 27 are the designed front editorial. Pages 28 to 83 are the Directors' Report, the auditor's material and the financial statements — near-identical typographic treatment year on year, and largely delivered pre-formatted out of the audit process. The automation target is roughly 24 pages, not 43.

Section drift is small and predictable. Across a full year the running order changed in six places, all of them renames or single-section swaps:

3   The approach: separate the three layers

Today the three layers are fused. Content lives in Word documents and email threads, numbers live in Excel, and the layout exists only inside an InDesign file that one person can open. Any change to any layer means going back to that one person. Separating them is the whole idea.

Layer 1 — Data
One workbook, one figure, one place

Every number in the report lives in a single Excel workbook with a fixed sheet layout — the same shape each year, so FY26's file is last year's file with new values. Racing statistics, prizemoney, wagering, participation, the Snapshot figures, award and premiership winners, feature race results. Nothing is typed into the layout.

Layer 2 — Template
A library of branded spreads that read the data

Each recurring spread type is built once, to the brand, and pulls its figures from the workbook. The KPI tables rebuild themselves. The charts redraw. Section copy sits in plain text files that any Comms author can edit without opening a design tool.

Layer 3 — Output
Print-ready PDF, plus clean handoff to the designer

The template produces a proofable PDF at any point in the cycle — including at first draft, when today there is nothing to look at but a Word file. It also produces the assets the internal designer needs in InDesign, in formats that place without redrawing.

4   How this fixes proofing

Fact-checking an annual report is slow because the same figure appears in several places and each appearance is typed independently. FY25 wagering turnover is the clearest example: total turnover of $7,610.8M and its 4.0% year-on-year fall appear in the Key Performance Indicators table, again on the Industry Snapshot page, and again in the Wagering narrative. Three chances to mistype, and three places a late restatement has to be chased down.

In the template that is one cell referenced three times. Change it once, it changes everywhere, and it cannot disagree with itself.

The bigger win

Every variance percentage is calculated from the two underlying values rather than supplied and typed. The FY25 KPI page alone carries more than sixty variance figures. None of them need checking again — they are arithmetic on numbers that have already been checked once.

6   What comes out, and what the designer still does

You asked for PDF plus an InDesign handoff, which is the right call for year one — it keeps the designer in control of the final artefact while removing the mechanical work from their plate.

The template produces:

  • A print-ready PDF of the front editorial, available from the first draft onwards. This is what changes the review cycle: reviewers see laid-out pages weeks earlier than they do now, so comments land on the real thing rather than on a Word file.
  • Charts as editable vector SVG. These place directly into InDesign as live artwork — the designer can restyle them, but nothing is redrawn and no number is retyped. This alone removes most of the chart-conversion work you described.
  • Tables and body copy as structured text for placement, so the InDesign build is flow-and-style rather than type-and-check.

The designer's time moves off tables and charts and onto the photographic and feature spreads — the pages where design judgement actually shows. If the template proves itself this year, FY27 can consider producing the front editorial PDF directly and using InDesign only for the cover and feature spreads.

8   What I need from you to start

  1. The FY25 InDesign source, or a high-resolution page export, so the template matches the existing look rather than reinterpreting it. The PDFs I have are enough to read structure from, not enough to match typography exactly.
  2. Whatever FY26 Excel files exist today, in whatever state they are in. Their real shape matters more than a tidy one.
  3. Confirmation on Gotham. The 2016 style guide specifies it; if the template is to render type directly we need it licensed for embedding, or a substitute agreed.
  4. A named owner per themed section, and the expected date for the audited financials.
  5. Fifteen minutes with the internal designer, so the handoff formats suit how they actually work rather than how I assume they do.

9   Honest limits

  • Year one costs more than it saves. Building the template takes longer than laying out one report. The return arrives in FY27, when the year's work is loading data and copy into something that already exists.
  • I cannot generate photography or illustration. Image selection and any new graphic assets stay with the designer. I can build diagrams and charts; I cannot make pictures.
  • The financial statements should stay out of scope. They are the majority of the page count but almost none of the design work, they arrive pre-formatted, and getting them wrong carries real consequence. Merge them as supplied.
  • Accessibility is a separate question. If a tagged, screen-reader-accessible PDF is required, that needs deciding now rather than at the end — it affects how the document is produced.
